# Reasoning Models

**Last reviewed:** September 15, 2026

> AI Use Disclosure: Codex was used to research, organize, and draft this guide.

Reasoning models are language models developed to work through problems using intermediate steps, often with additional computation while answering. They remain neural networks. Their training and use explain the distinction more clearly than a separate architecture label.

**Audience and prerequisites:** Readers who understand [tokens, generation, and assistant training](how-language-models-work.md). No programming required.

## What intermediate reasoning adds

An autoregressive model can generate intermediate steps and use them as context for later steps. **Chain-of-thought prompting** supplies examples of that behavior in the prompt. The [original research by Wei and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903) showed improvements on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning tasks under the studied conditions.

For an illustrative scheduling problem, a model could list the required meeting durations, work out which slots overlap, and check a proposed schedule against the constraints. Each intermediate result can help with the next decision. This is an invented example of a possible computation; the usefulness of the steps depends on whether they are correct.

Prompting a model to show steps changes its input. Training a reasoning model changes its parameters so that useful problem-solving behavior becomes more likely. Models without a dedicated reasoning label can also solve reasoning tasks, as the chain-of-thought experiments demonstrate.

## How models learn to reason

One route is **reinforcement learning**: generate attempted solutions, score their outcomes, and update the model toward behavior associated with higher reward. A math answer or a program's test results can provide a checkable signal. Such rewards still measure only what the checks cover.

The [original DeepSeek-R1 report](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12948v1) provides a concrete training example:

- **R1-Zero** applied reinforcement learning to an already pretrained base model without a preliminary supervised fine-tuning stage. The authors observed behaviors such as revisiting an approach, alongside readability problems.
- **R1** used a multistage process combining supervised examples and reinforcement learning.
- **Distilled models** learned from solutions generated by a stronger model. A reasoning model therefore need not receive all its capabilities through its own reinforcement-learning run.

Training from rewards, learning from demonstrations, and using a stronger model's outputs are compatible methods. The report establishes particular methods and measured results, rather than a universal account of how every provider trains its models.

## Training compute and inference compute

| When computation happens | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **During training** | Updates parameters using data and a training objective. |
| **During inference** | Uses the trained model on a particular request, potentially generating intermediate work, revisiting a candidate solution, or considering alternatives. |

**Inference-time compute**, also called test-time compute, is the work spent answering a request. Additional reasoning usually costs time and computation. In OpenAI's implementation, reasoning tokens occupy context space and contribute to billed output even when they are not shown to the user; supported models offer controls over reasoning effort. [OpenAI reasoning documentation](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/reasoning).

The practical question is whether additional work improves the result enough for the task. Compare answer quality, time, and cost on representative requests. A complicated scheduling problem and a simple text-formatting request can justify different budgets. More generated text alone does not establish better reasoning.

## Reasoning inside an agent loop

Reasoning and tool execution play different roles in the same system. Consider a hypothetical coding task:

| Step | What happens | Evidence available |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Inspect a failure | A file or test tool returns the relevant code and error. | Observations from the environment. |
| Develop a change | The model works through possible causes and proposes a patch. | A hypothesis to check. |
| Apply and test | The runtime executes editing and test tools. | An actual change and test output. |
| Revise or finish | The model interprets those results and chooses the next action. | Evidence supporting another attempt or completion. |

The model may reason between tool calls. Its expectation that a test will pass is separate from the test result returned by execution. The [agent-loop guide](../agent-loops-and-autonomy/how-agents-work.md#what-happens-inside-the-agent-loop) explains how the runtime connects these steps. The same distinction applies to looking up an order or checking a research source.

## What a reasoning trace can tell you

A displayed explanation, a reasoning summary, and internal model computation are different things. For example, [OpenAI's reasoning summaries](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/reasoning#reasoning-summaries) summarize reasoning without exposing its raw reasoning tokens. Visibility depends on the model and interface.

Even a detailed explanation can omit factors that influenced an answer. [Turpin and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388) demonstrated this by introducing biases into prompts: the tested models sometimes rationalized an answer without acknowledging the influence. Those experiments studied specific earlier models; they show why a plausible explanation alone cannot establish a faithful account of how an answer was produced.

For the coding example, assess the patch, test coverage, and observed results. For a sourced report, inspect the cited evidence. Intermediate reasoning can help produce a result, while those checks establish what the result actually supports.
